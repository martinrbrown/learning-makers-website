Built environment

Physical places like buildings, public areas and transportation must be designed so that people with disabilities can enter, move around and use them safely and on their own.

The universal design principles apply here as they were first created for buildings and spaces.

Building codes and regulations set a legal minimum. They define what is required, not what is ideal. Universal design goes further by aiming for spaces that work well for everyone, not just spaces that meet the lowest threshold the law requires.

A new office building includes a step-free entrance and lifts, but its accessible toilet is on a different floor from the main facilities. The space meets the building code but fails the spirit of universal design.

Accessibility in the built environment is not optional for most new construction in jurisdictions that follow the UN Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities (CRPD). It is a legal obligation derived from a human rights framework.

Retrofitting an existing building for accessibility can cost ten times more than including the same features in the original design. Inclusive design from the start is both cheaper and more equitable.

When reviewing a physical space for accessibility, check arrival, entry, circulation, facilities, and emergency egress separately — each has distinct requirements and common failure points.
